Repiping in Silver Spring, PA

Repiping is the planned replacement of the water supply pipes inside a house, either the whole system or one section of it. We send plumbers to Silver Spring, PA and nearby towns, and this page covers pipe behind your walls, not the buried line from the meter.

Signs your supply piping is wearing out

One symptom rarely settles the question. A pattern of them does.

  • Brown or orange water at the tap after the house has sat idle.
  • Pressure that has slipped year after year, even though the street supply is fine.
  • A second or third pinhole leak, or green crust on copper joints.
  • Plastic fittings that crack or snap when touched during other repairs.
  • Rust-stained threaded joints on steel pipe in the basement.
  • Old gray plastic piping, which many plumbers advise replacing.
  • A metallic taste that returns after the tap runs for a minute.

Inside the house or outside it: what repiping covers

Repiping replaces supply lines downstream of the main shutoff valve inside the house: the branches to sinks, showers, toilets, the water heater and appliances. The buried run from the meter, curb stop or private well to the house is a separate job, handled under water service line repair. A weak street connection and weak house piping can look alike at the tap, so sort out which is failing first.

Cities and boroughs founded in the 1700s, such as Lancaster, hold a mix of old and renovated houses. A home built decades ago can still have galvanized steel supply lines. That is general trade knowledge, and the only way to know what your house has is to look at the pipe.

Pipe materials compared

Plumbers argue about materials, so ask for the reasoning behind the one proposed for your house.

CopperA long track record, rigid runs, tolerates heatCost, soldering in tight spaces, and whether your water has been linked to pinholes. Ask your water utility for its annual quality report.

Whole house or partial: how to decide

Use the count and the pattern. A single failed joint in an otherwise sound run is a repair. Several failures in the same material, or failures that keep moving from one spot to the next, point toward replacing that whole run or the whole system. When a pipe has already split, see burst pipe repair for the immediate fix.

A partial repipe makes sense when the trouble sits in one area, such as the kitchen and bath on one side, and the rest is in good shape. Ask the plumber to mark on a sketch what will and will not be replaced, since mixed materials meet at transition fittings. If you suspect hidden damage first, a leak detection visit can show whether the problem is spread out or concentrated.

How a repipe is planned and carried out

  1. Walk-through and fixture count

    The plumber looks at pipe material, access, the water heater and every fixture before writing anything down.

  2. Written scope and material choice

    You get a document stating which lines are replaced, with which material, and what is left alone.

  3. Permit check

    Permit rules vary, so ask the township zoning and permits office or its building code inspector, and ask the plumber who files.

  4. Water shutoff plan

    Agree in advance when the water will be off and which fixtures will work between stages.

  5. New lines, connections and pressure test

    Fresh runs go in, fixtures reconnect, and the system is pressure tested before any wall is closed.

  6. Patching and final check

    Access openings are closed, and the plumber confirms flow and pressure at each fixture.

Living in the house during the work

Water is shut off for stretches while old lines come out and new ones connect. Ask for the schedule in writing, because a plan that moves room by room is easier to live with than a single long outage. Keep a few gallons for drinking, and plan showers and laundry around the shutoff windows.

Expect openings in drywall, ceilings or floors along the route. Whether patching and painting are in the quote varies between companies. Decide that before the contract is signed, not after the walls are open.

Galvanized, CPVC and copper: three common starting points

Galvanized replacement is the most common reason for a full repipe. The pipe rusts from inside, so flow shrinks even when it looks sound outside, and replacing it restores both pressure and water color.

CPVC that cracks tends to do so at fittings, sometimes years after installation. A single crack calls for a repair, but several suggest the system is aging together.

Copper pipe repair is practical for isolated damage. If a house shows pinholes in multiple spots, ask what is causing them before choosing the same material again.

Checking a contractor before you sign a repipe contract

Pennsylvania requires contractors doing $5,000 or more of home improvement in a year to register with the Attorney General, and the registration number must appear on contracts and estimates. A written contract is required once the work costs more than $500. A contractor may not demand payment before the contract is signed.

You can generally rescind a home improvement contract within three business days of signing. Verify any plumber, us included, at attorneygeneral.gov or by calling 1-888-520-6680.

What drives a repipe estimate

A repipe estimate rises with the number of fixtures, the number of floors and how hard the walls are to open. A small house with open basement access is a different job from a large one with plaster and finished ceilings. A clear price before work starts should list material, patching and permits separately.

Number of fixtures and floors

Every fixture needs its own branch, and each floor adds vertical runs.

Pipe material

Copper, PEX and CPVC differ in material cost and in how long each takes to install.

Access to the walls

Unfinished basements and open ceilings go faster than plaster, tile or tight attic spaces.

Patching and finishing

Drywall repair and paint may be included or handled by another trade.

Permits and inspection

Permit rules vary, so ask the township or borough office what applies to your house.

Planned replacement is a different matter from water damage after a leak, and coverage varies by policy, so ask your insurer, and photograph any damage you want considered.
